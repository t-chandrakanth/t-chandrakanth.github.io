The dashboard says 97% auto-match. The number is true. It is also the least
informative number in the building.

Behind it, a month of items that did not match sits in a queue, and the queue
does not behave like a tail. A tail is the same problem, harder. This is not
that. Pull fifty items off the top and the same handful of shapes come back over
and over: an invoice settled in two instalments, one wire covering thirty-one
invoices net of two credit notes, a receipt three pounds light after a currency
conversion, an item booked on the last day of a period and settled on the first
day of the next.

None of these is a hard case. Every one of them is a case an analyst resolves in
minutes by reading two screens and applying a rule they could write down on an
index card. The matcher could not, because the matcher was asked a question that
none of these items is an answer to.

And the queue is only the visible half. The other half of the failure is inside
the 97%: items the engine matched confidently to the wrong counterpart, which
nobody will ever look at, because the entire point of an auto-match is that
nobody looks at it.

## Why widening the rules does not converge

The instinct is to add tolerance. A tolerance for the currency case. A subset
rule for the aggregated case. A special window for period ends. Each of these is
locally correct, and the aggregate is a rule set that grows faster than the
problem it covers.

Start from what a deterministic matcher actually computes. For a candidate pair
of records $a$ (a ledger item) and $b$ (a settlement record) it evaluates a
conjunction

$$
m(a, b) \;=\; \big[k(a) = k(b)\big] \;\wedge\; \big[\lvert v(a) - v(b) \rvert \le \epsilon\big] \;\wedge\; \big[\lvert t(a) - t(b) \rvert \le \delta\big]
$$

where $k$ is the reference key, $v$ the signed amount, $t$ the date, and each
square bracket is one when the condition inside it holds. It then resolves the
surviving pairs into an assignment subject to $\sum_b x_{ab} \le 1$ and
$\sum_a x_{ab} \le 1$: at most one $b$ per $a$, at most one $a$ per $b$.

Widening a tolerance does not make a match more likely to be right. It makes
more pairs eligible. If settlement records for the counterparty arrive with local
density $\rho$ per unit of currency per unit of time near $(v(a), t(a))$, the
expected size of the candidate set for $a$ is

$$
\mathbb{E}\,\lvert C(a) \rvert \;\approx\; 4\,\epsilon\,\delta\,\rho
$$

which is the product of the two tolerance widths. Double the amount tolerance
and double the window, and the candidate set quadruples. The engine still has to
choose one, and it chooses by whatever secondary ordering it was configured with,
which is usually date, which is usually arbitrary.

The rule set is not converging either. With $c$ recurring classes, and items that
can present any subset of them at once — a short-paid invoice, netted into an
aggregated remittance, across a period boundary, in a second currency — a rule
set that adjudicates class by class has $2^c - 1$ contexts it must be correct in.
At $c = 7$ that is 127, and the seventh rule is written by someone who has never
read the first.

## What the matcher assumes without saying so

Three assumptions are doing all the work in that expression, and none of them
appears anywhere in the configuration.

The first is *same value*: the amount on one side equals the amount on the other.
The second is *same window*: the two events happened close enough together on a
single measure of time. The third, and the one that is almost never articulated,
is *one-to-one*: the correspondence between ledger items and settlement records
is a bijection on the matched subset. One invoice, one payment.

Enterprise settlement is not a bijection. It is a many-to-many correspondence
between sets, sometimes with sign changes, sometimes spanning a reporting
boundary, and the keys that identify its members are rewritten in transit. Stated
properly, the object being recovered is a pair of subsets

$$
A \subseteq \mathcal{A}, \quad B \subseteq \mathcal{B} \quad \text{with} \quad \Big\lvert \sum_{a \in A} v(a) \;-\; \sum_{b \in B} v(b) \Big\rvert \;\le\; \epsilon
$$

which is subset-sum wearing a business suit, one of Karp's original twenty-one
NP-complete problems. A rule engine cannot approximate its way there by adding
tolerances, because tolerance is a parameter of the wrong model.

<KeyLine>The residual is not the hard tail of the same problem. It is seven different problems the matcher was never posed.</KeyLine>

The seven classes below are not seven bugs. They are the seven ways enterprise
settlement routinely violates one of three assumptions the matcher was never told
it was making.

## The seven classes

A matcher applies its predicate in a fixed order: block on the reference key to
build a candidate set, compare amounts, compare dates, then resolve what survives
into a one-to-one assignment. Four gates. An item is reported at the first gate
that rejects it, and that gate is frequently not the assumption that actually
broke.

That gap is why the tolerance always gets attached to the wrong place. A split
settlement is a cardinality failure reported as an amount mismatch, so an amount
tolerance is what gets added, and an amount tolerance does not fix cardinality.
It only widens $\epsilon$ until the wrong things match.

Every amount in the seven sketches below is invented.

### 1. Split settlement

An invoice for 88,400.00 is settled by two transfers: 60,000.00 on the 14th and
28,400.00 on the 22nd. Both carry the invoice reference. Both are perfectly
legible to a human.

The key gate passes, because the reference survived. The value gate rejects both
transfers, because neither equals the invoice. The engine emits three exceptions
for one event: two unapplied receipts and one invoice still open at 88,400.00.
Nobody reading the queue can see that they are the same item, because the queue
is a list of records, not a list of events.

The assumption that broke is one-to-one. The gate that fired was value. Widening
$\epsilon$ far enough to cover a 28,400.00 gap is not a tolerance, it is an
abdication, so nobody does it, and the class stays in the queue permanently.

### 2. Aggregated remittance

A single wire of 412,905.66 settles thirty-one invoices, net of two credit notes,
from a counterparty that pays on a fortnightly cycle. The remittance advice
arrives separately, as a spreadsheet attached to an email, in a layout the sender
changes without notice. Sometimes it does not arrive.

The wire has no usable key: the reference field carries the payer's own batch
number. The value gate rejects it against every open invoice on the account. What
the engine reports is one exception. What is actually unresolved is thirty-three.

Some engines add a subset rule that looks for the combination of open items
summing to the wire. With four hundred open items on the account this is
subset-sum, and engines that attempt it either cap the search depth, which misses
the true combination, or accept the first combination within tolerance, which
finds a false one.

### 3. Currency conversion and rounding drift

An invoice is raised for 47,318.20 in the counterparty's currency. The payer
converts at their own rate on their own value date, the receiving bank applies a
spread, and the sub-ledger was revalued at the month-end rate. The receipt lands
3.41 away from the carrying amount. The same class covers allocation remainders:
a 100.00 charge split three ways is 33.33, 33.33 and 33.34, and the third one
never matches anything.

Here the violated assumption and the firing gate agree. Value equality is
genuinely false, and the value gate says so. This is the only class of the seven
where a tolerance is the correct instrument.

It is also the class that poisons the other six. Set $\epsilon$ to 5.00 to absorb
the drift and every open item within 5.00 of the receipt becomes a candidate. On
a high-volume account that is not one item.

### 4. Timing across a period boundary

A payment is initiated on the 31st, value-dated the 1st, and posted to the bank
statement on the 2nd. The corresponding ledger item was booked in the period that
closed on the 31st. Nothing is wrong with any of these dates. They are four
different and individually correct answers to the question *when*.

The window gate rejects the pair at $\delta = 3$ days and accepts it at
$\delta = 10$. That looks like a free fix and is not, because a ten-day window at
a period boundary reaches into a period that is already closed, already reported
and already signed. The tolerance that repairs the match breaks the accounting,
and it does so quietly, because a matched item generates no exception and nobody
re-opens a closed period to go and check.

### 5. Reference-field mutation

An invoice leaves the billing system as `INV-2026-0088412`. The payment file
format truncates the reference to twelve characters. The counterparty's payables
system substitutes their own purchase-order number. What arrives in the bank
statement narrative is `PO 44120/88412 PART`.

This class fails differently from the others. It does not produce a bad
candidate, it produces *no* candidate, because the key gate is also the blocking
step. The symptom is a null result, which is why this class is systematically
over-represented in queues triaged by exception message rather than by cause.

Approximate string matching recovers a large share of it, and that recovery is
precisely what the record-linkage literature has been about since Newcombe's work
on automatic linkage of vital records in 1959 and the Fellegi and Sunter
probabilistic framework in 1969. It also introduces the failure that literature
spends most of its time on: a confident match to a neighbouring reference.

### 6. Partial settlement and short pay

An invoice for 12,600.00 is paid at 12,180.00. The 420.00 difference is not
drift. It is a deduction: a damaged-goods claim, an unearned early-payment
discount, a disputed freight charge, a promotional allowance the counterparty has
decided to take.

Structurally this is class 3. The value gate rejects it, the assumption violated
is value equality, and the two shapes are indistinguishable to the engine. The
difference is intent, and intent is not a field.

That is why this is a control problem and not a matching problem. Any amount
tolerance wide enough to absorb currency drift absorbs small deductions too, and
absorbs them silently. The item closes, the receivable clears, and the write-off
never reaches anyone with the authority to approve it. The engine has not made a
matching error. It has made a policy decision on nobody's behalf.

### 7. Reversal and rebook

An item is posted, reversed the following day, and reposted with corrected coding
under a new document number. Three records, one economic event, and a net of zero
across the first two.

The payment, when it arrives, references the original document, which is now
closed. The key gate rejects it. But the violation underneath is arity: three
ledger records correspond to one settlement, and the matcher has no
representation for that at all.

The degradation here is the worst in the taxonomy. If the rebook carries the same
amount as the original, which it usually does because only the coding changed,
then the reversal is a perfect amount-and-date match for some *other* open item
of the same value. The engine can close two items, both wrongly, and because the
pair nets to zero every control total still agrees. A balance identity is a
necessary condition and not a sufficient one, which is a
[failure that recurs wherever an identity is used as a validity check](/writing/neural-models-and-accounting-identities).

Two things in that table matter more than the headline.

The first is the inversion. Currency drift is 29.3% of the queue and 6.2% of the
effort: high volume, trivial to clear. Aggregated remittance is 13.6% of the
queue and 31.8% of the effort. A dashboard that ranks classes by queue depth
ranks them almost exactly backwards, and an automation programme that follows the
dashboard spends its budget on the cheapest class in the book.

The second is what the table does not contain. Draw 1,000 items at random from
the 232,800 that auto-matched and have an analyst re-derive each one from source.
Suppose six come back wrong. That is a point estimate of 0.6%, with a 95%
interval of roughly 0.2% to 1.3%, and 0.6% of 232,800 is about 1,400 items —
more than four of the seven exception classes, in a population that by
construction nobody inspects. There is no queue for these. The only way to see
them is to go looking, which is the
[evaluation-without-ground-truth problem](/writing/evaluating-agents-without-ground-truth)
wearing different clothes.

## Limits

Three honest ones.

The taxonomy is descriptive and I have not built the classifier. Assigning an
unseen exception to one of seven classes is itself a matching problem, the
boundaries blur — a short-paid invoice, netted into an aggregated remittance,
across a period boundary is three classes and one item — and until that
assignment is automatic the taxonomy is a way of thinking rather than a control.

The classes are not mine. Record linkage has a sixty-year literature: Newcombe's
automatic linkage of vital records, the Fellegi and Sunter probabilistic
framework, Winkler's string comparators and expectation-maximisation estimation
at the US Census Bureau, Christen's synthesis of blocking and entity resolution.
The many-to-many amount problem is subset-sum, and one-to-one resolution is the
assignment problem Kuhn solved in 1955. What I claim is the framing: that these
seven failure modes are violations of exactly three unstated assumptions, and
that the gate reporting a failure is often not the assumption that broke.

The numbers are the weakest part of the piece. The item counts are plausible; the
analyst-minute distribution is a guess, and it is the guess the whole argument
about misallocated effort rests on.

## Where this goes

The next piece in this pillar takes the reframe seriously. If the object being
recovered is a correspondence between subsets, across systems and across time,
then reconciliation is
[a graph problem](/writing/reconciliation-is-a-graph-problem), and the seven
classes are seven edge types rather than seven exceptions. The rest of the pillar
sits on the [research page](/research).

The question worth holding onto is not what your auto-match rate is. It is this:
of the items that auto-matched last month, how many has anyone ever checked?
